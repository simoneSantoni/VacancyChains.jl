## 1. Introduction

The vacancy chain model, first formulated by Harrison C. White in his landmark 1970 work *Chains of Opportunity: System Models of Mobility in Organizations*, remains one of the most elegant structural frameworks for analysing mobility in social systems. White's fundamental insight was deceptively simple: rather than tracking the movement of people, one could track the movement of *vacancies* through a system of ranked positions. When a person leaves a position, the resulting vacancy propagates in the opposite direction of the people who fill it, creating a "chain" of sequential moves that terminates only when the final vacancy is filled by a new entrant or abolished entirely.

This conceptual inversion—treating the vacancy rather than the person as the active agent—opened up new avenues of inquiry across multiple disciplines. Following White's death in May 2024, there has been renewed scholarly interest in his foundational contributions, including a special feature in *Sociologica* (2025) devoted to his intellectual legacy. Simultaneously, the economics profession has experienced a remarkable resurgence of interest in vacancy chain mechanisms, driven by a new wave of structural models of the labour market and the availability of matched employer-employee datasets.

This report provides a comprehensive review of the vacancy chain literature across the two disciplines—sociology and economics—that have engaged most deeply with the concept. It traces the historical development of the idea, charts recent theoretical and empirical advances, and identifies open questions and emerging frontiers. The aim is to inform a research design that brings White's structural vision into conversation with contemporary data infrastructure, particularly for a UK-based study leveraging the new Linked Employer-Employee Data (LEED) spine.

## 2. Foundational Contributions

### 2.1 White's Original Formulation (1970)

White's *Chains of Opportunity* grew out of an empirical puzzle: how do mobility patterns within bureaucratic organisations (churches, corporations, civil services) actually work? White modelled the system as a finite set of ranked positions. When a position at the top becomes vacant (through retirement, death, or expansion), it triggers a chain of moves as each vacancy is successively filled by someone from a lower position. The chain terminates when the last vacancy is filled by a new recruit from outside the system (an "entry" or "sink") or when the position is simply abolished.

The mathematical backbone of White's model was a Markov chain, with the transition matrix describing the probability that a vacancy in stratum *i* is filled by someone from stratum *j*. The key summary statistic was the **multiplier effect**: the expected total number of moves generated by a single initial vacancy. White derived this from the fundamental matrix of the absorbing Markov chain, showing that a single retirement at the top of the Methodist Church hierarchy could generate, on average, several downstream moves.

The model had three defining features that distinguished it from the individualistic attainment models (Blau and Duncan, 1967) that dominated mobility research at the time. First, it was **structural**: mobility was a property of the system of positions, not of the attributes of individuals. Second, it was **relational**: the probability of any given move depended on the configuration of vacancies across the entire system. Third, it was **demand-driven**: vacancies created by the system's structural needs—not the aspirations of individual actors—governed the rate and pattern of mobility.

### 2.2 Early Extensions in Sociology (1970s–1990s)

White's model was rapidly applied beyond its original ecclesiastical setting. Stewman and Konda (1983) extended vacancy chain analysis to large-scale organisations, demonstrating that chain-based models of internal labour markets could predict promotion patterns with considerable accuracy. Stewman's subsequent work on organisational demography (1986, 1988) showed how the age structure of an organisation interacted with vacancy chains to produce cohort-specific career opportunities.

In the housing market, White himself (1971) proposed a "multiplier" framework for residential vacancy chains: the construction of one new luxury dwelling triggers a chain of moves as each household "trades up," eventually freeing a low-income unit for a first-time buyer. Lansing, Clifton, and Morgan (1969) and later Marullo (1985) tested this "filtering" hypothesis empirically, finding that the extent of trickle-down was more limited than the model predicted—a finding that would resurface in the 2020s housing literature.

Chase's *Annual Review of Sociology* article (1991) provided the definitive stock-taking of the first two decades of vacancy chain research, noting its extension to contexts as diverse as national labour markets, the historical development of professions, gender and ethnic discrimination in job and housing markets, and even the shell acquisition behaviour of hermit crabs. Chase emphasised that vacancy chains appeared to organise social processes in remarkably similar ways across human and animal populations, suggesting deep structural regularities.

### 2.3 Hedström and the Mechanisms Turn

Peter Hedström's 1992 paper "Organizational vacancy chains and the attainment process" in the *Journal of Mathematical Sociology* marked a pivotal intellectual moment. Working with Swedish manufacturing data, Hedström recast vacancy chains as a *social mechanism* in the sense later codified in his influential *Social Mechanisms* volume (1998, with Swedberg). Rather than simply fitting a Markov model to observed transitions, Hedström asked: under what structural conditions do vacancy chains generate unequal attainment outcomes? His answer was that the **thickness** of an organisation's hierarchy—the relative number of positions at each level—determined whether resources allocated through chains would percolate broadly or concentrate narrowly.

This "mechanistic" reading of White's model had profound implications. It meant that vacancy chain analysis was not merely a descriptive technique for tracing mobility flows; it was an *explanatory* framework that identified the structural conditions under which opportunity cascades through a system. This is the interpretation that most directly informs the current project.

## 3. Vacancy Chains in Economics

### 3.1 Early Economic Treatments

Within economics, engagement with vacancy chains was slower to develop. Akerlof, Rose, and Yellen (1988) used the concept to explain the procyclicality of job-to-job quits: in tight labour markets, employed workers receive more outside offers, generating longer chains of replacement moves. However, their model treated the unemployment rate as exogenous, severing the link between vacancy chain dynamics and aggregate labour market outcomes.

The broader "job flows" literature (Davis and Haltiwanger, 1992; Davis, Faberman, and Haltiwanger, 2006) documented the simultaneous creation and destruction of jobs at the firm level. While this work established the empirical importance of worker reallocation, it did not model the sequential, chain-like structure of replacement hiring that White's framework emphasised.

### 3.2 The Mercan–Schoefer Breakthrough (2020)

The modern economics literature on vacancy chains was catalysed by Mercan and Schoefer's 2020 paper in *American Economic Review: Insights*, "Jobs and Matches: Quits, Replacement Hiring, and Vacancy Chains." Their contribution was to integrate vacancy chains into the canonical Diamond–Mortensen–Pissarides (DMP) search and matching framework. In the standard DMP model, all job openings arise from new job creation; when a worker quits, the match is destroyed and the job disappears. Mercan and Schoefer showed that this assumption was at odds with survey evidence: approximately 56 per cent of vacancies in the German IAB survey were classified by employers as **replacement hiring** into existing positions, not as new job creation.

Their augmented model introduced **sunk job creation costs**: once a firm invests in creating a position (an office, a machine, an organisational blueprint), that position persists even after the worker quits. The firm then has an incentive to repost the vacancy rather than close the position. This generates an endogenous vacancy chain: one worker's quit leaves behind a valuable vacant position, which is reposted, potentially filled by another employed worker who in turn leaves behind *their* position, and so on. "Vacancies beget vacancies." The procyclicality of quits—workers are more likely to quit in booms—thereby amplifies business cycle fluctuations, because each quit triggers a cascade of replacement hiring.

A further contribution by Mercan, Schoefer, and Sedláček (2024), published in the *American Economic Journal: Macroeconomics*, extended this work into a "congestion theory" of unemployment fluctuations in which new hires and incumbent workers are imperfect substitutes, achieving a tenfold increase in the volatility of hiring within an otherwise standard search model.

### 3.3 Elsby, Gottfries, Michaels, and Ratner (2025)

The most substantial recent contribution is Elsby, Gottfries, Michaels, and Ratner's paper "Vacancy Chains," published in the *Journal of Political Economy* (2025, Vol. 133, No. 11, pp. 3550–3604). This paper develops a full equilibrium model of firm dynamics with on-the-job search and multi-worker establishments. The key innovation is a three-region structure: at any given time, firms occupy one of three "regions" depending on their marginal product of labour relative to their current employment level.

In the **expansion region**, firms actively create new positions. In the **replacement region**, firms maintain their current scale by replacing workers who quit. In the **natural wastage region**, firms allow their workforce to shrink by not replacing departing workers. Vacancy chains are initiated by new positions created in the expansion region; they propagate recursively as positions in the replacement region are filled by workers poached from other firms lower in the productivity hierarchy; and they terminate when a position is finally filled from either the natural wastage region or the unemployment pool.

The authors derive a **sufficient statistic for chain length**: the ratio of workers in the replacement region to those in the natural wastage region plus the unemployed. This statistic is itself governed by the ratio of vacancy-filling rates, linking the micro-mechanism of chain propagation to observable macro aggregates. Quantitatively, the model reconciles the large dispersion of labour productivity across establishments with the empirical volatility and persistence of job creation and unemployment.

### 3.4 Gianelle and Tattara: Empirical Chain Computation (2014)

Gianelle and Tattara (2014) provided the first direct empirical computation of vacancy chains using matched employer-employee micro data. Drawing on a longitudinal database covering all manufacturing employees in the Veneto region of Italy (1982–1996), they developed a transparent algorithm for identifying chains from observed job-to-job transitions.

Their findings were striking. Vacancy chains accounted for more than one-third of total hires on average. Both the number and average length of chains were clearly **pro-cyclical**: during booms, more chains were initiated and each chain involved more links. Workers were systematically sorted along chains according to skill and bargaining power, with higher-status workers at the "head" (the initiating vacancy) and lower-status workers at the "tail" (the terminal move). There was a well-defined wage progression from tail to head, confirming that the vacancy's "downward" movement through the job hierarchy mirrored the "upward" movement of workers—precisely as White's original model predicted.

### 3.5 Danish Evidence: Bagger, Fontaine, Galenianos, and Trapeznikova (2022)

Bagger et al. (2022) used comprehensive Danish data merging online job advertisements with matched employer-employee records and firm-level revenue data. Their contribution was to establish the **micro-level empirical relevance of replacement hiring and vacancy chains** at the firm level. They found that a one standard deviation increase in the separation rate was associated with a doubling of the vacancy rate. Crucially, separations to employment had more than three times the effect on vacancy-posting than separations to non-employment—consistent with the vacancy chain mechanism, whereby quit-driven departures are more likely to trigger replacement hiring than layoffs or retirements.

## 4. Vacancy Chains in the Housing Market

### 4.1 The Filtering Hypothesis

White himself (1971) extended the vacancy chain framework to housing markets, proposing that the construction of new high-income dwellings would trigger a cascade of residential moves that would eventually "filter" down to benefit low-income households. This "residential multiplier" became a cornerstone of market-oriented housing policy arguments: if you build at the top, vacancies will percolate to the bottom.

Early empirical work (Lansing, Clifton, and Morgan, 1969; Sands and Bower, 1974; Marullo, 1985) found that residential vacancy chains were real but often shorter than the theory predicted. Chains frequently "leaked" as units were absorbed by in-migrants, demolished, or converted to other uses before reaching the lowest-income segments. Turner (2008) and Turner and Wessel (2019) used administrative data from Stockholm and Oslo to estimate Markov chain models of housing vacancy transfers, finding that the distributional benefits of new construction depended heavily on the type and location of the initial development.

### 4.2 The New Wave: Mast (2021), Bratu et al. (2023), and Gilbert (2024)

The 2020s have seen a resurgence of vacancy chain analysis in housing economics, driven by the availability of geo-coded population registers and address-history databases.

**Mast (2021)**, published in the *Journal of Urban Economics*, used address-history data from Infutor Data Solutions to track the sequences of moves sparked by 686 new market-rate multifamily buildings in 12 large US central cities. He found that 67 per cent of initial movers came from within the same metropolitan area, and that approximately 70 per cent of chains initiated by a new high-end unit reached a below-median-income neighbourhood before terminating—a strong empirical validation of the filtering mechanism.

**Bratu et al. (2023)** used geo-coded population-wide register data from the Helsinki Metropolitan Area, showing that new centrally-located market-rate housing supply triggered moving chains that quickly reached middle- and low-income neighbourhoods and individuals, even in the short run. This was the first study to use full-population administrative data to document housing vacancy chains at the unit level, rather than relying on neighbourhood-level transitions.

**Gilbert (2024)**, in a Harvard working paper, used confidential Census Bureau data on the residential histories of the entire US population from 2000 to 2021 to identify 1.5 million vacancy chains initiated by new housing construction. His finding—that suburban new construction had limited effects on urban affordability—demonstrated that the geography of vacancy chains matters as much as their length. Chains tend to propagate within, not across, housing submarkets, so suburban supply does little for urban low-income households.

## 5. Emerging Domains and Extensions

### 5.1 Public Administration and Leadership Turnover

An emerging stream of work applies the vacancy chain framework to leadership turnover in public sector organisations. When a senior public manager departs, the resulting chain of replacements can cascade across multiple agencies and jurisdictions. This application connects White's structural sociology to classic public administration concerns about institutional knowledge, succession planning, and organisational stability. It is particularly timely given the high rates of federal vacancy documented in the US (where over 80 Senate-confirmed positions were vacant more than 50 per cent of the time between 2009 and 2023) and the aging workforce in UK local government.

### 5.2 Ecological and Biological Systems

Chase and colleagues (Chase, 1991; Weissburg, Roseman, and Chase, 1991) demonstrated that vacancy chains operate in animal populations, most notably in the shell-acquisition behaviour of hermit crabs. When a larger shell becomes available (analogous to a "new position"), it triggers a cascade of shell swaps as each crab trades up. The isomorphism between human and animal vacancy chains suggests that the underlying mathematical structure—the absorbing Markov chain—captures a general principle of sequential resource allocation that transcends any particular social context.

### 5.3 Digital Labour Markets and Scraped Data

The proliferation of professional profile data (e.g., Revelio Labs, Lightcast) has opened new possibilities for vacancy chain research. Revelio Labs, which aggregates and de-identifies professional profiles and resumes, provides longitudinal career trajectories with monthly resolution—making it possible to infer chains of job-to-job transitions at a global scale. However, these data are subject to well-known biases toward white-collar and knowledge-economy workers, limiting their applicability to the full-population vacancy chains that White's model envisions. Lightcast, which primarily aggregates job postings, captures the demand side (new vacancies entering the system) but cannot track the sequential replacement moves that constitute the chain itself.

### 5.4 Urban and Regional Development

Persky and Felsenstein (2008) advocated for vacancy chain ("job chain") models as a superior alternative to conventional economic impact multipliers in urban and regional analysis. Their argument was that standard input-output multipliers measure only the monetary flows generated by new investment, while job chain models track the actual sequences of employment improvements experienced by real workers. This distinction matters especially for evaluating "trickle-down" claims about economic development projects: do the benefits of a new factory actually cascade to disadvantaged workers, or are they captured by in-migrants and commuters?

## 7. Theoretical Tensions Between Disciplines

### 7.1 Structure vs. Equilibrium

The most fundamental tension between the sociological and economic treatments of vacancy chains lies in their ontology. In sociology, following White, vacancy chains are elements of a *structural* account of mobility: the system of positions constrains and channels movement, and individual attributes are secondary. In economics, vacancy chains are integrated into *equilibrium* models: the chain length and propagation speed are endogenous outcomes of firms' optimising behaviour and workers' search strategies. Mercan and Schoefer, and especially Elsby et al., explicitly derive vacancy chains from the interaction of sunk costs, on-the-job search, and firm heterogeneity—a very different intellectual register from White's Markov-chain formalism.

This is not merely a difference of language. The structural perspective implies that vacancy chains are shaped by institutional rules (promotion ladders, seniority norms, hiring conventions) that exist independently of individual optimisation. The equilibrium perspective implies that chain dynamics will adjust endogenously to changes in the economic environment. A study that can simultaneously test both perspectives—using institutional variation in the UK's LEED data alongside the equilibrium predictions of the Elsby et al. model—would make a significant theoretical contribution.

### 7.2 The "Sink" Problem

A second tension concerns the treatment of chain *termination*. In White's model, chains end cleanly when a vacancy is filled by a new entrant (from outside the system) or when a position is abolished. In the economics literature, termination is governed by the probability that a vacancy is filled from the unemployment pool rather than through poaching—itself a function of the tightness of the labour market. In the housing literature, chains "leak" when units are absorbed by in-migrants, newly formed households, or conversions. These different "sink" structures have important implications for the measurement and comparison of chain lengths across studies and contexts.

### 7.3 Opportunity vs. Inequality

White titled his book *Chains of Opportunity*, reflecting an optimistic reading: vacancy chains are mechanisms through which opportunities cascade down the social hierarchy. The housing literature has been more sceptical, with decades of research questioning whether filtering truly benefits the poor (Ratcliff, 1949; Galster, 1996; Clark, 2010). The economics literature is largely neutral on distribution, focusing instead on aggregate efficiency and business cycle dynamics. A sociological study using LEED data could bridge this gap by directly measuring whether vacancy chains reproduce or ameliorate pre-existing social stratification.

### 7.4 Micro-Data vs. Aggregate Calibration

A methodological tension exists between the empirical approaches of the two disciplines. Sociologists and applied labour economists (Gianelle and Tattara, 2014; Bagger et al., 2022) identify chains directly from micro-data, tracing the actual sequence of worker moves. Macro-economists (Mercan and Schoefer, 2020; Elsby et al., 2025) calibrate structural models to aggregate moments (quit rates, vacancy rates, the Beveridge curve) and derive chain dynamics as equilibrium implications. The direct computation approach is more faithful to White's original vision but is data-intensive and subject to identification challenges; the calibration approach is more parsimonious but abstracts away from the social heterogeneity that makes chains sociologically interesting.

## 8. Open Questions and Research Frontiers

### 8.1 Full-Population Chain Computation in Labour Markets

Despite the theoretical maturity of the vacancy chain concept, there remain remarkably few studies that directly compute chains from full-population administrative data. Gianelle and Tattara (2014) used regional manufacturing data; Mast (2021) and Gilbert (2024) worked with address histories in the housing domain. The availability of the UK's LEED spine—covering approximately 30 million payrolled employees per month—creates an unprecedented opportunity for a national-scale vacancy chain computation in the labour market. No such exercise has been conducted to date for the UK.

### 8.2 The "Social Physics" of Chain Length

What determines how long a vacancy chain is? The economics literature identifies the distribution of firm productivity as a key determinant (Elsby et al., 2025). The sociology literature points to organisational thickness and the institutional rules governing promotion (Hedström, 1992; Stewman, 1986). An empirical study that simultaneously measures chain length and the characteristics of the firms, workers, and institutional environments through which chains pass would make a significant contribution to both literatures.

### 8.3 Vacancy Chains and Social Inequality

Are vacancy chains equalising or stratifying? If chains initiated by high-status departures tend to terminate before reaching lower-status workers, then the cascade of opportunity is "truncated" and the stratification structure is reinforced. Conversely, if chains are long and reach deep into the occupational hierarchy, they serve as conduits of upward mobility. This question has been explored in housing (Mast, 2021; Bratu et al., 2023) but remains largely untouched in the labour market. Census linkages and education data (e.g., the UK's LEO dataset) could provide the "demographic" variables needed to test this directly.

### 8.4 Cross-National Comparative Studies

No study has systematically compared vacancy chain dynamics across countries with different labour market institutions. The availability of comparable matched employer-employee datasets in multiple countries (the UK's LEED, Brazil's RAIS, Denmark's IDA, Italy's WHIP) creates the conditions for a comparative institutional analysis. Do countries with stronger employment protection have shorter chains? Do centralised wage-bargaining systems produce more or less "percolation" of opportunity? These questions are at the frontier of the literature.

### 8.5 The Role of the Informal Sector

In developing economies, a substantial share of employment is informal and therefore invisible to administrative data. In Brazil, for example, RAIS captures only formal-sector employment, meaning that vacancy chains appear to terminate whenever the last link is filled by someone from the informal sector. This creates a systematic bias toward measuring chains that are shorter than they actually are, since the "true" chain may continue through a sequence of informal-sector moves that are unobserved. Addressing this bias—perhaps through survey supplements or linked household data—is essential for extending vacancy chain analysis to the Global South.

## 9. Implications for Research Design

Drawing on this review, a vacancy chain study based at Bayes Business School should consider the following design principles.

**First**, the **UK LEED spine** is the most promising dataset for a labour-market vacancy chain study that aspires to publication in top sociology or economics journals. Its universal coverage eliminates the sampling problem that truncated vacancy chains in earlier datasets like ASHE. Its monthly frequency allows the precise sequencing of moves that chain identification requires. Collaboration with John Forth and the Wage and Employment Dynamics (WED) project provides an institutional gateway to this data.

**Second**, a **dual-track validation design** that combines LEED (for universal coverage and validity) with Revelio Labs data (for occupational titles, skills, and seniority markers) would address the main limitation of administrative tax data: the absence of "soft" variables that capture the social meaning of each move. This hybrid approach is novel and would strengthen the paper's contribution in either a sociology or management journal.

**Third**, the study should be framed around the **inequality question**: do vacancy chains reproduce or ameliorate social stratification? This framing is the strongest bridge between the sociology of White and Hedström, the economics of Elsby et al. and Mercan–Schoefer, and the policy relevance demanded by the ONS's "public good" accreditation requirements. Linking LEED to Census 2021 data or LEO education records would enable a direct test of whether certain demographic groups are systematically excluded from the longest (highest-opportunity) chains.

**Fourth**, the study should explicitly engage with both the **structural** (sociological) and **equilibrium** (economic) interpretations of vacancy chain dynamics. The UK's institutional landscape—minimum wage legislation, regional variation in labour market tightness, sector-specific norms—provides the variation needed to distinguish between these perspectives empirically.

**Fifth**, **target journal strategy** should inform data and framing choices:

| Target Journal | Ideal Dataset | Framing |
|---|---|---|
| *American Sociological Review* / *American Journal of Sociology* | UK LEED + Census | How vacancy chains reproduce social inequality and systemic stratification |
| *Administrative Science Quarterly* | Revelio Labs (via WRDS) | How organisational status and skills dictate the speed and length of job cascades |
| *Journal of Political Economy* / *Econometrica* | UK LEED | Testing the sufficient-statistic predictions of Elsby et al. (2025) at scale |
| *Journal of Urban Economics* | LEED + geographic identifiers | Spatial propagation of vacancy chains across UK regions ("Levelling Up") |

## 10. Conclusion

The vacancy chain concept, first articulated by Harrison White over half a century ago, is experiencing a remarkable renaissance across multiple disciplines. In economics, the integration of vacancy chains into equilibrium search models has produced new insights into the sources of labour market volatility and the aggregate implications of replacement hiring. In housing studies, population-wide register data have enabled the most rigorous tests to date of the filtering hypothesis. In sociology, the posthumous reassessment of White's legacy has renewed interest in the structural foundations of mobility.

Yet for all this progress, the central sociological question that animated White's original work remains unanswered at scale: *how do vacancy chains distribute opportunity across the social structure?* The convergence of universal administrative data (LEED), new theoretical frameworks (Elsby et al., 2025), and the institutional resources available at Bayes Business School creates a unique moment to answer this question for the UK's national labour market.
